Poverty Persists Even When People Are Working

Across many communities, families are working hard but still remain one illness, disaster, or weak market week away from deeper poverty. The data below shows how widespread low-security work has become and why this keeps household income unstable.

  • 20.6 million workers are estimated in openly informal arrangements, including self-employed, unpaid family workers, and domestic workers.
  • 3.3 million family workers are unpaid, signaling severe income insecurity at household level.
  • 2.3 million domestic workers remain exposed to weak and inconsistent protection coverage.

Source context: IBON Foundation analysis of official labor data (2025); BARMM Economic Briefer (Issue 4, 2024).

The Reality of Informal Work

A majority of workers in the Philippines face poor working conditions, low productivity, and exclusion from basic legal protections.

Scale & Prevalence

Dominating the Economy

Informal workers dominate the Philippine economy, especially in rural areas and regions like BARMM. Many of these jobs evade minimum wage laws and employment standards.

This high rate of low-paid, temporary jobs locks millions of Filipinos in a cycle of poverty, severely capping their prospects for upward mobility.

Vulnerability

Income Instability & Lack of Benefits

Reliance on seasonal or market-driven work leads to uncertain earnings and financial exclusion. Such workers face life’s challenges completely exposed.

Scale of exposure: Around 20.6 million workers are in openly informal work categories.

Unpaid labor: About 3.3 million family workers receive no direct wage income.

Domestic work segment: Roughly 2.3 million workers remain in jobs with uneven protection coverage.

Economic fragility: Earnings drop quickly during weak sales, disasters, or health shocks.

Systemic effect: Without stable protection, temporary setbacks become long-term poverty traps.

Connections to Vulnerability

How weak job quality and low protection push households deeper into poverty.

Lack of Social Protection

Informal workers miss essential safeguards, leaving them without insurance or safety nets during downturns.

Limited Upward Mobility

Low skills, poor training access, and weak bargaining power trap workers in low-productivity roles.

Crisis Vulnerability

During crises like COVID-19, informal workers suffer disproportionate job and income losses due to unsafe conditions.

Market Dependence

Exposure to fluctuations without collective representation or access to baseline financial services.

Globalization's Impact

Trade openness drives contractualization and labor flexibilization. Export growth shifts demand toward low-skilled production jobs, which increases low-wage informality and fuels dependence on Overseas Filipino Workers (OFWs).

As domestic formal opportunities shrink, vulnerability to global fluctuations rises.

Structural Causes

  • Trade Openness Shifts demand toward low-skilled production jobs while displacing stable domestic industries.
  • Labor Flexibilization Prioritizes contractualization and temporary labor over permanent job security.
  • OFW Dependence Lack of decent domestic jobs forces workers to seek risky opportunities abroad.
  • Weak Enforcement Failure to adequately enforce the Bangsamoro Labor Code and similar worker protections.

Human Effects

  • Stalled Upward Mobility: Trapped in low-productivity, low-skill roles without training access.
  • Missing Social Protection: No health insurance, retirement funds, or safety nets during sickness or disability.
  • Disproportionate Harm: Suffering massive job and income loss during events like the COVID-19 pandemic.

"The decent work deficit ensures that the most vulnerable populations absorb the shocks of an interconnected, volatile global market with no safety net to catch them."

Policy Implications

Approaches for formalization, social protection, and resilience.

  • Urge formalization via simplified business registration and bureaucratic ease.
  • Encourage legal recognition for informal jobs to bring them into the regulatory fold.
  • Ease transition pathways for businesses operating in the "gray" economy.
  • Create adaptable social security grids encompassing self-employed and temporary workers.
  • Prioritize health insurance and emergency cash transfers designed for non-traditional labor.
  • Alleviate financial exclusion with credit access designed for seasonal incomes.
  • Ensure strict compliance with the Domestic Workers Act to extend protections to vulnerable groups.
  • Uphold and heavily enforce regional decrees like the Bangsamoro Labor Code.
  • Combat illegal contractualization loopholes.
  • Build training access and skills development to help workers command higher bargaining power.
  • Facilitate smooth transitions to formal jobs through subsidized certification programs.
  • Address educational gaps for unskilled labor displaced by global trade.

A World Guaranteed for All

Poverty in the Philippines is perpetuated by the decent work deficit resulting from vast informal employment. By prioritizing legal recognition, targeted social protections, and easing transitions to formal work, we can build an economy where basic safety, opportunities, and dignity are guaranteed for all—not just the privileged few.
